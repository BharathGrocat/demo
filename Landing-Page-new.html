Chapter 02

How they invest

Families are neither aggressive nor overly cautious. They prefer calculated risk, put long-term compounding first, and now see global investing as a natural next step. Given a free hand, founders and second-generation leaders turn first to public markets; the third generation would rather buy businesses outright.

56%put long-term capital appreciation and compounding first

Chapter 03

How they decide

Big decisions are moving from a single voice to a shared process. The most common model is family and professional management deciding together. Each generation wants a different model: founders prefer family-led with advisors, the second generation professional management with oversight, and the third a hybrid.

30%say family values guide decisions through informal family consensus

Chapter 04

Preparing the next generation

Involvement does not fade with each generation. It dips in the second, as families lean on structure, then rises sharply in the third: families hold the reins tightest during the second handover, then loosen their grip. Succession is planned more often in spirit than on paper: 42% have broad alignment, and 36% a clear, documented plan.

54%of third-generation families say the next generation already influences or co-leads decisions

Chapter 05

Building the family office

A family office usually begins as a practical answer to complexity: too many assets, entities and jurisdictions. Over time it becomes an institution of governance. Families want it to professionalise investment management, talent and processes first, then diversify across geographies and asset classes.

Summary

Across every theme, the next generation is not simply inheriting wealth; it is inheriting responsibility.

Preservation remains the shared instinct, but families now pursue it alongside growth. Governance once carried in one leader’s judgement is being set down in charters, committees and mandates. The third generation is stepping back into the business, and the family office is maturing into an institution of governance. Wherever a family sits today, the direction of travel is consistent: towards structure, shared stewardship and a next generation that is prepared, not just present.

FAQ

About the study

How the study was done

Conversations with family business leaders and wealth custodians across first, second and third generations.

Families across India with wealth of approximately INR 100 crore to INR 10,000 crore.

Findings are illustrative and not derived from a statistically validated survey.

1. What is Custodians of Change?

Custodians of Change: The Next Generation of Indian Wealth is a September 2026 study by Grant Thornton Bharat and Entrust Family Office. It draws on conversations with family business leaders and wealth custodians across generations, representing wealth from approximately INR 100 crore to INR 10,000 crore, on values, governance, succession, risk and purpose.

2. What are the top long-term priorities for Indian family enterprises?

Preserving family wealth, values and continuity is the top priority, cited by 78% of the custodians we spoke with, regardless of generation or net worth. Preservation is no longer the whole story: almost half also want to grow and diversify income beyond the primary business, and nearly 40% are building a legacy beyond financial returns.

3. How involved is the next generation in Indian family business decisions?

Involvement follows a cycle rather than a steady decline. In third-generation families, 54% say the next generation already has meaningful influence over decisions or co-leads parts of the business, compared with 18% in second-generation families, where families tend to hold the reins most tightly before loosening their grip by the third.

4. How many Indian business families have a documented succession plan?

About a third. In the study, 36% of families have a clear, documented succession plan, while the most common position, at 42%, is broad family alignment that has not yet been formalised. Informality largely disappears at the highest wealth levels, and leaders over 50 are far more likely to have formalised their plans.

5. How do Indian families approach investment risk?

Families favour calculated risk over high-stakes bets or avoiding risk altogether. Long-term capital appreciation and compounding is the investment outcome that matters most for 56% of respondents, a preference that holds across generations and age groups, while thematic and purpose-led investments are generally seen as complementary rather than core.

6. Where do Indian families invest when they invest globally?

Indian families favour financial markets over physical assets abroad. Global public equities, private markets and listed funds lead, cited by 44%, followed by international fixed income and capital-preservation strategies at 19% and direct stakes in overseas businesses at 17%. Interest in international real estate, lifestyle assets and collectibles is lower.

7. What triggers setting up a family office in India?

The most common trigger, cited by 24% of families, is a deliberate shift from running the operating business to managing a diversified portfolio. Other catalysts include managing diversified interests after a major liquidity event and investments expanding across asset classes, entities or jurisdictions, each cited by 21%.

8. What governance model do Indian family businesses prefer?

It depends on the generation. Founders favour a family-led model with external advisors, second-generation leaders lean towards professional management with family oversight, and third-generation custodians settle on a hybrid. Across families, the most common way big decisions get made is a joint process, while close to 20% say their approach varies by decision.

9. What would families change if they built their family office again?

Strengthening the use of technology and reporting systems tops the list, at 17%, followed by a greater role for independent professionals at 13%. Governance structure and decision rights, and ownership and holding structures, follow closely at 12% each, while capital allocation and liquidity planning feature further down.

10. How was the Custodians of Change study conducted?

The study is based on conversations and interviews with a select group of Indian family business leaders and wealth custodians across generations, representing wealth of approximately INR 100 crore to INR 10,000 crore. Its findings and archetypes are illustrative and are not derived from a statistically validated survey.
